01 / Pass the grunt test

Study the three questions a visitor should be able to answer quickly: what do you offer, how will it make my life better, and what do I need to do to get it?

Notice how clarity depends on the first impression, not on the amount of information available somewhere deeper in the site.

02 / Remove villainous noise

Miller treats a website as an elevator pitch rather than a clearinghouse for every fact a company could say about itself.

Mark the difference between detail that resolves a customer's uncertainty and detail that makes the main promise harder to find.

03 / Guide the customer toward action

A clear promise needs a visible path: the page should show the customer what to do next and make that action feel connected to the problem being solved.

Study how direct calls to action, transitional calls to action, images, and proof work together without competing for the same attention.

04 / Audit one page

Choose one website and write the answers a first-time visitor should find immediately: the offer, the improved life or outcome, and the next step.

Then remove or demote one piece of noise, strengthen one proof point, and make one primary action unmistakable.

02 / The Principle

Make the path to value easy to see.

A page earns attention by making the customer's problem, the promised improvement, and the next step legible before the surrounding detail gets in the way.

One example

A specialist studio replaces a homepage collage and a paragraph of services with one sentence naming the costly problem it solves, three proof-backed outcomes, and a clear invitation to book a diagnostic call. Visitors who need the service can orient themselves before exploring the full portfolio.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Open one website as if you were a first-time customer. Write the offer, the better outcome, and the next action you can infer in five seconds. Circle the biggest source of noise, then propose one edit that makes the primary path easier to follow.

03 / Field Notes

Four current signals on making the next step obvious.

Marketing Dive / 2026-11-06

Lands' End turns one clear idea into an always-on experience

What happened: Lands' End opened a Chicago Holiday Tree Farm pop-up built around archival copy about providing 'everything but the tree.' The stations connect to outerwear and pajamas, while the retailer plans to reuse the experience as content across the holiday season. Why it matters: The activation has a simple customer-facing idea and a visible connection to the products, so the experience is not just an event looking for a brand afterward. A clear frame can make many channels feel like parts of one promise. Watch: Watch whether the reusable content and local participation create stronger product consideration, not only social reach or a memorable installation.

OpenAI / 2026-11-05

Australian universities make AI practice part of the work

What happened: OpenAI described collaborations with Australian universities that pair access to ChatGPT Edu and Codex with clinical simulations, teacher-training practice, hackathons, industry projects, and explicit rules for when AI is allowed, prohibited, or required. Why it matters: The offer is clearer when capability is attached to a concrete job and a supervised practice loop. Access to a tool is only the opening promise; the user needs a visible path from trying it to producing work that matters outside the classroom. Watch: Watch whether these programs measure better decisions and real project outcomes, not just usage or the number of AI-enabled assignments.

Meta Newsroom / 2026-11-06

Meta makes a half-formed request into a reusable personal space

What happened: Meta introduced Spaces in Muse: users describe an idea such as a gift tracker, planner, or client hub, then keep revising the resulting experience, connect services, and place it in the app where it fits their routine. Why it matters: The product reduces the distance between a vague need and a usable workflow. The important promise is not that an AI can generate a first version, but that the user can return, refine it, and see the tool stay connected to the job. Watch: Watch which spaces are revisited and retained after the first surprise, and how often users need to repair a generated workflow before trusting it.

TechCrunch / 2026-11-02

Trajectory sells a learning loop, not just an AI model

What happened: Trajectory raised $45 million for a platform that lets organizations connect models to application feedback and continually improve them for specialized work. Its interface is designed to let nontechnical users define what good looks like and turn that feedback into model improvements. Why it matters: This is a useful product-positioning distinction: the offer is not abstract intelligence but a repeatable path from observed failure to a better result. The clearer promise is tied to a behavior customers can recognize inside their own workflow. Watch: Watch whether customers can see measurable improvement in specialized tasks and whether human feedback remains part of the operating loop rather than a launch-story detail.

05 / The Practice

Let clarity carry the customer forward.

State the offer plainly, show the better outcome, remove competing noise, and give the right customer one obvious next step.
