01 / Find the hidden job

A choice can perform a psychological job that is invisible in a purely functional description. Start by asking what reassurance, control, identity, or meaning the person is seeking.

Notice which apparently irrational behaviours become sensible once the hidden job is included in the model.

02 / Study the placebo mechanism

Placebo effects show that expectation and interpretation can change experienced outcomes even when the physical intervention is held constant.

Mark the cues that make an intervention feel credible, active, safe, or worth attending to.

03 / Separate hacking from deception

A useful psychological intervention works with a real human response; it does not require the designer to make a false claim about what happened.

Ask where a ritual or signal helps people access a beneficial state and where it merely exploits attention without delivering value.

04 / Design one supportive intervention

Choose one product, service, or experience. Write the hidden job, the expectation or cue shaping it, and one small intervention that could make the desired behaviour easier.

State what you would measure to distinguish a genuine improvement in experience from a temporary novelty effect.

02 / The Principle

Treat hidden psychology as part of the product.

People do not experience an offer through physical function alone. Expectation, ritual, status, reassurance, and meaning can change what the offer does for them; design these layers honestly and test their effect.

One example

A health app could begin a difficult habit with a short, consistent ritual that signals care and readiness. The ritual does not pretend to provide treatment; it helps the user enter the state in which the useful behaviour is easier to begin.

03 / Field Notes

Four current signals on designing the cues, rituals, and systems that shape experience.

Google / 2026-11-10

Google gives hard work a distinct model identity

What happened: Google made Gemini 4 Carbon available for complex, multi-step coding, cybersecurity, scientific reasoning, and other long-horizon agentic workflows, while positioning Argon as the choice for most ordinary work. Why it matters: The model name and the explicit division of labor are cues that help people decide when to invoke deeper reasoning. The product is not only a capability; it is also a mental shortcut for matching task difficulty to the right mode of work. Watch: Watch whether users actually route difficult, verification-heavy work to Carbon, or whether the distinction remains launch language without becoming a durable workflow habit.

Apple / 2026-11-10

Apple turns accessibility into a discoverable viewing ritual

What happened: Apple announced a Sign Language option for supported Apple TV titles, designed and tested with Deaf and native signers, with an icon, settings control, and searchable collection to make the feature findable. Why it matters: The value is carried by more than the underlying interpretation: a label, a setting, and a dedicated collection turn an inclusive capability into a clear expectation users can act on. The cue reduces the work of discovering whether a story is truly available to them. Watch: Watch whether the icon and search language become trusted signals of a consistent experience across titles, rather than a promise whose availability is hard to verify.

Marketing Dive / 2026-11-11

Maybelline updates a familiar phrase as perceptions change

What happened: Marketing Dive reported that Maybelline is updating its long-running “Maybe she’s born with it. Maybe it’s Maybelline.” line as beauty perceptions evolve, with a punctuation and phrasing change intended to feel more inclusive and empowering. Why it matters: A small verbal change can alter the psychological job of a famous line: from explaining beauty as an innate condition to making the brand feel more aligned with present-day agency and self-definition. Familiarity becomes design material rather than a reason to freeze the message. Watch: Watch whether the revised line preserves the memory structure that made the original valuable while changing the interpretation people carry into the product experience.

Retail Dive / 2026-11-05

Holiday shoppers want AI help without surrendering the choice

What happened: Retail Dive reported that holiday shoppers are using AI for price comparisons and narrowing gift ideas, while still wanting autonomy in the buying process. Why it matters: The useful intervention is not maximum automation. A recommendation system can provide reassurance and reduce search effort while preserving the ritual of choosing, which is part of the emotional value of gifting and the buyer’s sense of agency. Watch: Watch whether commerce assistants make the next decision easier without making the shopper feel displaced, rushed, or unable to inspect the reasoning behind a suggestion.

05 / The Practice

Design for the whole human response.

The best intervention may change expectation, attention, or meaning before it changes the underlying thing. Find that leverage carefully, then prove that it helps.
