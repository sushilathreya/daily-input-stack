Make value feel rare before it is gone.

In Chapter 7 of Influence, Robert Cialdini explains why scarcity increases perceived value, how competition intensifies desire, and why urgency can distort judgment.

Apprenticeship Note

Scarcity is not value by itself. It is a signal that can focus attention and intensify desire, but the signal becomes dangerous when urgency replaces judgment about what the thing is actually worth.

01 / Notice the rule of the few

Cialdini begins with a simple pattern: opportunities seem more valuable when they are less available.

Study the difference between a genuinely limited resource and a manufactured restriction, and notice how both can change attention before the underlying offer changes.

02 / Track threatened freedom

When access is restricted, people often experience the loss of choice as a threat to freedom and want the restricted option more intensely.

Mark where the chapter's examples show reactance, and separate the desire to possess something from the desire to recover an option that seems to be disappearing.

03 / Watch competition narrow judgment

Scarcity becomes more powerful when people must compete for what is scarce; emotional arousal can then outrun careful evaluation.

Notice how rivalry, deadlines, and changing availability can make an offer feel urgent even when the buyer has not established its actual value.

04 / Audit one urgency claim

Choose one offer that uses limited quantity, limited time, exclusive access, or a countdown. Write what is genuinely constrained and what is merely framed as constrained.

Then rewrite the urgency so it remains truthful, helps the right customer decide, and does not ask pressure to substitute for proof of value.

02 / The Principle

Use scarcity to reveal value, not replace it.

A real constraint can help people prioritize, but scarcity becomes manipulative when it creates urgency without giving the customer enough evidence to judge the underlying value.

One example

A small workshop limits enrollment because each participant receives live feedback. Its message names the cohort size, explains the feedback constraint, and closes registration when the seats are filled instead of resetting an artificial countdown.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one current offer. List its real constraints, the evidence of value, and the emotional pressure created by its urgency language. Remove one exaggerated cue and replace it with a truthful explanation of what becomes unavailable and when.

Marketing Dive / 2026-11-04

Target and Walmart turn holiday selling into competing stories

What happened: Marketing Dive reports that Walmart is using its 'Who Knew?' platform and a Muppet Christmas Carol campaign to change perceptions, while Target is using a gifting academy to reassert its cheap-but-chic difference. Both are moving beyond fragmented deal messages toward a single seasonal narrative. Why it matters: In a price-sensitive category, the brands are making the value proposition easier to remember by giving it a recognizable frame. Story is doing the work that a louder discount claim cannot: helping shoppers understand why this retailer should matter. Watch: Watch whether the narratives improve consideration and conversion, not just recall, and whether the promised difference remains visible in stores, online, and delivery.

JCPenney answers deal fatigue with a map, not another countdown

What happened: JCPenney launched a holiday campaign built around real shoppers, a weekly deals cheat sheet, and simple ways to save. The article says only 32% of shoppers completely or mostly trust retailers to offer a fair or competitive price, while JCPenney is trying to make its promotions easier to navigate. Why it matters: When every offer claims urgency, the useful differentiator becomes legibility. A recurring guide can reduce the customer's search cost and make a real saving easier to inspect than a pile of rotating promotions. Watch: Watch whether the cheat sheet increases profitable basket-building and trust, or merely trains shoppers to wait for the next promotion.

Meta makes an AI feature feel valuable by giving it a place to live

TechCrunch / 2026-11-06

AI usage rises while spending growth flattens

What happened: TechCrunch reports Ramp data showing token volumes rising while AI spending growth flattened in late summer; customers are using cheaper models, routers, budgets, and defaults to control costs. The piece frames this as a buyer's market shaped by price competition rather than a collapse in usage. Why it matters: More usage does not automatically mean more perceived value or more revenue. Buyers are learning to separate the outcome they need from the most expensive available capability, which makes evidence of task-level value more important than scale rhetoric. Watch: Watch whether new agent workflows create measurable outcomes that justify spend, or whether efficiency improvements keep pushing usage up while unit economics move down.
