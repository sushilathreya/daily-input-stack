Do not borrow a name when you need a position.

In Chapter 11 of Positioning, Al Ries and Jack Trout show how a familiar name can carry inherited recognition while failing to give a new offer a distinct place in the mind. The discipline is to separate borrowed equity from a position the buyer can actually remember.

01 / Familiarity can disguise strategic debt

A well-known name appears to offer a shortcut into the market, but its associations were earned by an earlier product, category, or promise.

Ask what the new offer receives immediately and what it must spend effort explaining because the inherited meaning points somewhere else.

02 / Borrowed equity is not a new position

Recognition may win the first glance without telling the prospect what this particular offer stands for. The mental handle has to identify the new choice, not merely recall the old one.

Notice the difference between entering memory and occupying a useful place inside it.

03 / The name changes the comparison frame

A borrowed name can force the new offer to be judged against the parent brand or its established competitors, even when the intended strategy requires a different comparison.

Study which comparison the name makes automatic and whether that comparison helps the buyer understand the offer.

04 / Make the extension earn its own meaning

The practical question is not whether a name is famous. It is whether the new offer can build a clear, credible association that survives once the inherited familiarity fades.

Look for the cost of asking one name to carry incompatible promises, audiences, or categories.

05 / Audit one borrowed position

Choose one extension, sub-brand, or product family. Write the inherited association, the new promise, and the single position the buyer should remember.

Stop with one recommendation: keep the name, narrow the promise, or create a clearer handle for the new offer.

02 / The Principle

Make the new offer earn its own mental slot.

A familiar name can reduce the cost of being noticed, but it cannot substitute for a clear position. Separate inherited recognition from the promise the buyer should attach to this offer.

One example

A trusted cleaning brand that launches a premium home-security service may gain an initial glance from the parent name, but the buyer still needs a distinct reason to remember the new offer: what category it belongs to, what risk it removes, and why this name is credible there.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Pick one brand extension. Write the parent brand's inherited meaning, the extension's intended category, and the one association it should own. If those three do not align, rewrite the promise or recommend a clearer name.

TechCrunch / 2026-10-20

ChatGPT adds privacy as a visible product boundary

What happened: OpenAI added Locked Chats to ChatGPT, letting users protect selected conversations with a PIN or device biometrics and place them in a separate section. The feature does not change retention, encryption, or model-improvement settings. Why it matters: The product is making a familiar name carry a more specific promise: not simply private, but visibly separated at the moment another person may use the device. The boundary is useful because it tells the user what protection exists and what it does not change. Watch: When a product inherits broad trust, look for the concrete boundary that makes the new promise memorable, and state the limits beside the benefit.

Watch: Separate the inherited reach of a platform or parent brand from the repeat behavior the new product can generate on its own.
