01 / Treat the formula as a promise carrier

Caples's formulas are useful because they force the headline to carry a recognizable selling idea instead of settling for atmosphere or cleverness.

As you read, name the concrete promise each pattern makes before judging its style.

02 / Match the pattern to the reader's motive

A formula only works when its structure fits what the intended reader already wants to gain, avoid, understand, or become.

Notice which patterns lead with benefit, problem, curiosity, authority, or proof, and what kind of attention each one assumes.

03 / Specificity keeps formulas from becoming filler

A familiar headline shape can still be weak if its wording leaves the benefit vague. Specific details give the structure something worth carrying.

Mark the words that turn a general claim into a result, audience, situation, or consequence the reader can recognize.

04 / Adapt the structure to the offer

Caples offers patterns, not substitutes for judgment. The writer still has to select the angle that fits the product and the prospect's state of awareness.

Ask what would become misleading if you copied a formula without changing it for the actual offer.

05 / Rewrite one live headline

Choose one formula and use it to produce a headline whose promise is visible before the reader reaches the body copy.

Keep the formula only if it clarifies the reader's stake. If it merely makes the line sound familiar, discard it.

Close Reading Sequence

  1. What reader motive does each formula make easiest to express: gain, relief, curiosity, urgency, proof, or status?
  2. Which formula becomes stronger when you add a specific audience, situation, or result?
  3. Where could a formula create a misleading promise if the writer copied its shape without understanding the offer?
  4. Choose one live offer. Which formula best fits the reader's current problem, and what evidence supports that choice?
  5. Write one formula-based headline, then identify the exact words that carry its reason to continue.

02 / The Principle

Structure attention around a concrete promise.

A headline formula earns its place when it helps the right reader recognize a meaningful benefit, problem, or possibility quickly. The pattern supplies leverage; the offer and the reader's motive supply the substance.

One example

Instead of "A Better Way to Run Your Team," a project-management tool might use a problem-led structure: "How to See the Work That Is About to Slip Before Friday." The formula matters only because it makes a concrete risk visible.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one live offer and write three headlines using three different formulas: benefit, problem, and curiosity. Ask a colleague which reader motive each line activates, then keep the version whose promise is clearest.

Marketing Dive / 2026-10-16

Advertising Week puts the budget holder back in the room

What happened: Marketing Dive's Advertising Week New York roundup says the event's central questions were how brands connect with Gen Z and Gen Alpha, how AI is changing advertising, and whether creator marketing is cost-effective. It also notes the event was deliberately aligning itself with budget holders. Why it matters: The signal is a reminder that a message has two audiences: the person who experiences it and the person who approves the spend. A strong promise has to make the user's value visible while giving the buyer a reason to believe the channel and execution deserve budget. Watch: When evaluating creator or AI-led campaigns, look for language that joins audience response to an accountable business outcome rather than treating attention as the finish line.

Retail Dive / 2026-10-16

Halloween demand is shrinking as shoppers cut the extras

What happened: Retail Dive reports a LendingTree survey of 2,000 U.S. consumers found planned Halloween spending fell 16% year over year to $162, while 61% of shoppers plan to skip some purchases and 24% expect to use debt. Gen Z and millennials are the most likely to finance the occasion. Why it matters: The customer is not rejecting the occasion; they are editing it. A concrete offer that protects the experience while removing an expense can be more persuasive than a generic seasonal discount. Watch: Watch whether seasonal messaging names the item or effort customers can safely leave out, instead of assuming the bigger basket is the only route to growth.

Retail Dive / 2026-10-14

Retail outlook improves, but the customer is splitting in two

What happened: Retail Dive reports Moody's upgraded the global retail and apparel outlook to stable, forecasting 2%-4% sector revenue growth over the next 12-18 months. The report still expects demand to be increasingly bifurcated, with upper-income spending holding up while lower- and middle-income shoppers focus on value and essentials. Why it matters: A market-level positive headline can conceal radically different customer realities. Positioning becomes more credible when the promise names whose situation is improving and what trade-off the offer removes for them. Watch: Read broad growth claims against the income or use-case segment underneath them; 'the consumer' is often too vague to support a trustworthy promise.

One fragment from the timeline worth carrying into the work.

Eric Seufert / following / 2026-10-19

A product fragment on turning capability into behavior

This fragment is useful because it names the behavioral question behind a new capability. The important test is not whether the feature is impressive, but whether it changes a repeated workflow enough for the user to adopt it.

I've always held that comparatively short CMO tenures were a function of the general inability of many marketing teams to clearly quantify (or even articulate) the revenue impact of their work. The erosion of deterministic identity on mobile forced app developers to confront the need for this a few years ago; web-based businesses mostly kicked the can down the road on this with ITP, but their reckoning seems to be materializing with tracking-domain blocking in iOS 27. The workflow adaptations that effective marketing teams implement to modernize measurement are, fundamentally, projects in data science and econometrics: designing experiments, executing them, and evaluating their results with analytical rigor. But designing a useful, worthwhile, and perceptibly productive experiment requires an undertanding of why *what you might learn* is valuable to the business. This doesn't necessarily mean that some new process, tool, feature, channel, measurement approach, etc. has an immediate revenue impact. But the longer-term or more abstract the impact, the more clearly and fluently the CMO needs to be able to articulate the benefits in the parlance of the CFO / CEO while also understanding the underlying mechanisms. Very few people survive that Venn diagram! And the proliferation of AI marketing solutions means those decisions become a) more numerous and b) thornier to assess with functional evals. These decisions range from cheap (adopting a new creative production tool) to enormously costly and distracting (hiring a consulting firm to integrate MMM into the measurement apparatus). None of these decisions are objectively good or bad on their own, but they can fail simply because the CMO can't articulate a vision for why they might contribute to the business and how that can be quantified over time. Make the reader's next reason obvious.
