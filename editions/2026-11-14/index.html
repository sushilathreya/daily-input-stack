01 / Let tempo compound learning, not noise

The chapter opens with high-tempo football to make a precise point: more plays create more opportunities to learn, but only when the team can interpret what happened and adjust.

Notice the qualification. Speed is valuable because it increases the rate of learning, not because activity is inherently virtuous.

02 / Build a cycle that turns uncertainty into work

Ellis and Brown organize growth around a continuous loop of analysis, ideation, prioritization, testing, and renewed analysis.

Study how the sequence prevents a team from jumping straight from a hunch to implementation while still keeping the work moving at a practical cadence.

03 / Prioritize ideas before the test queue fills

A large idea pipeline is useful only if the team has a disciplined way to choose what deserves scarce engineering, design, analytical, and attention capacity.

Mark how the ICE framework and collaborative nomination process make opportunity cost visible instead of allowing the loudest idea to win by default.

04 / Make valid results part of the operating rhythm

The testing rules treat experimental quality as a growth constraint: weak design, false positives, and inconclusive results consume opportunities that cannot be recovered.

Notice how the growth meeting closes the loop by reviewing metrics, unfinished tests, lessons, owners, and the next cycle's candidates.

05 / Store learning so the team does not repeat itself

The knowledge base is more than documentation. It lets the team search past tests, revisit conclusions, and avoid spending a new cycle rediscovering an old result.

Choose one current experiment process and ask what evidence would still be findable six months from now—and what disappears as soon as the meeting ends.

02 / The Principle

Design the loop that turns tests into memory.

Growth compounds when every experiment leaves the team with a sharper model of what to do next. Build a cadence that protects both speed and validity, then make the learning searchable.

One example

A product team does not treat its A/B tests as isolated launches. It reviews the North Star metric, ranks ideas by impact and confidence, records the result with its assumptions, and uses the finding to select the next experiment.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Map one growth loop in five steps: analyze, ideate, prioritize, test, learn. Identify the step where work currently stalls, the evidence it lacks, and one change to the weekly cadence that would make the next cycle more reliable.

03 / Field Notes

Four current signals on making experimentation a repeatable learning system.

Google / 2026-11-10

Google makes multi-step usefulness part of the phone proposition

What happened: Google's November Pixel Drop added Gemini Intelligence capabilities on Pixel 10, including multi-step tasks such as ordering coffee, proactive suggestions, message polishing, and Pixel Tag availability. Why it matters: The product story is organized around recognizable jobs rather than intelligence as an abstract claim. That gives the team testable adoption questions: which task earns a first use, whether the handoff succeeds, and which successful action creates a reason to return. Watch: Watch task completion and repeat use by job, not just feature awareness; a broad launch can still fail if the first multi-step experience is unreliable or hard to understand.

OpenAI API Changelog / 2026-11-13

OpenAI turns access control and latency into product experiments

What happened: OpenAI's November changelog listed enhanced role-based access controls, new GPT-5.1-Codex models for the Responses API, and extended prompt-cache retention of up to 24 hours. Why it matters: These are operating-system improvements that make it easier to run controlled work at scale: the right people can access the right actions, and repeated context can be tested with lower latency and cost. Growth here depends on shortening the distance between a useful workflow and the next iteration. Watch: Watch whether teams use the controls and cache economics to run more reliable workflow experiments, or whether additional capability simply increases setup complexity.

Marketing Dive / 2026-11-12

Fetch makes repeated attention the unit of a branded series

What happened: Fetch launched a six-part holiday microseries with a brand partner tied to each episode, offering viewers points redeemable for gift cards when they watch and shop through the app. Why it matters: The format creates a concrete loop—content, viewing, reward, shopping—that can be measured episode by episode. It tests whether brands can earn repeat participation through a useful system rather than relying on a one-off creative claim or a narrow fan base. Watch: Watch completion, repeat viewing, shopping conversion, and reach separately; high activity from reward-seeking viewers would not by itself prove durable brand learning or broad growth.

Google DeepMind / 2026-11-10

Google DeepMind benchmarks the visual work agents actually face

What happened: Google DeepMind introduced the Multimodal Knowledge Agent benchmark with Artificial Analysis to evaluate agents on visually complex files and real-world knowledge-work tasks such as interpreting blueprints, schematics, reports, and spreadsheets. Why it matters: The benchmark turns a vague promise—agents can handle knowledge work—into observable task conditions. That is the same discipline as a good growth cycle: define the job, make the result measurable, and use failure modes to choose the next test. Watch: Watch whether benchmark performance predicts useful outcomes in live workflows, including error recovery and time to a trusted next action, rather than only scores on a curated file set.

04 / Collected Fragments

One fragment from the timeline worth carrying into the work.

Davide Grieco / for_you / 2026-11-13

Test the message before polishing the campaign

Davide Grieco argues that growth teams should test ICP, pain point, and use case before investing in polished creative. The useful fragment is its sequencing claim: use speed to find a promising narrative, then spend quality effort on what earns evidence.

Every growth marketer I know who got fired over the last 12 months made the same mistake: they optimized for quality instead of speed. They spend three months designing the perfect landing page, filming high-production videos, designing fancy ads, and building out a full nurture flow. Then they launch. It flops. And they start over. In growth, 80% of your initiatives will fail. So if you dedicate resources to making each one beautiful, you're allocating unnecessary effort to initiatives that won't exist in three months. Test ICP → pain point → use case. Iterate until you find early signs of success. Then improve the quality of the campaign.

05 / The Practice

Make every experiment improve the next question.

A fast testing system earns its speed by converting results into shared judgment, not by producing a larger pile of activity.
