Give the name a place in the mind.

In Chapter 9 of Positioning, Al Ries and Jack Trout treat the product name as a memory handle: a short verbal cue that helps an offer occupy a clear place in the prospect's mind. The right name makes the position easier to remember, repeat, and defend.

Apprenticeship Note

A name is not a neutral label placed on a finished strategy. It can help a position enter memory, or make the offer harder to classify; choose the verbal handle with the prospect's mind in view.

01 / The name is a memory hook

The authors frame the name as the hook that hangs a brand on the product ladder in the prospect's mind. Naming therefore belongs inside positioning work, not at the end of it.

A useful name helps the prospect connect the offer to a category, benefit, or distinction quickly enough for the position to survive the noise of competing messages.

02 / Choose for the prospect's associations

The relevant test is not what the name means to the people who invented the product. It is what the word causes the prospect to see, expect, and remember.

A name can make a product feel familiar and legible, or make it sound like an inside joke whose intended meaning never reaches the market.

03 / Descriptive names can establish a foothold

A strong, almost-generic name can signal the territory an offer intends to occupy and make imitation harder for competitors to frame as the original.

The tradeoff is that a name closely tied to one position may constrain later expansion. That constraint is strategic only if the initial position is worth owning.

04 / Confusion is a positioning cost

Names that are too technical, too common, or too similar to a larger competitor force the prospect to spend scarce attention figuring out what the offer is.

The no-name problem begins here: when the verbal handle does not create a distinct mental slot, the product has to fight to be remembered before it can be chosen.

05 / Turn the name into a position brief

Choose a name by stating the category, the position it should cue, the associations it should invite, and the competitor or confusion it must avoid.

Stop with one brief that makes the name's intended mental work explicit. The test is whether someone can repeat the name and recover the position without a long explanation.

Close Reading Sequence

  1. What position does the name make easier to retrieve, and what does it make harder to imagine?
  2. Which associations belong to the prospect rather than to the product team that chose the name?
  3. When does a descriptive or generic-like name create a useful foothold, and when does it become a constraint?
  4. Choose one offer: what competitor, category, or mental confusion must its name overcome?
  5. Write a naming brief with the intended position, the associations to invite, the confusion to avoid, and the test for recall.

02 / The Principle

Make the name retrieve the position.

A name earns its strategic role when it gives the prospect a short, distinctive path back to the position you want the offer to own.

Example

A team-inbox product that wants to own fast shared response could choose a name that cues a clear communication space rather than an internal technical architecture, then test whether prospects can explain its category and difference after one exposure.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one offer and write three possible names. For each, state the position it cues, the association it creates, the confusion it risks, and the sentence a prospect should be able to repeat after hearing it once.

03 / Field Notes

Four current signals on how names and promises become the interface for trust, discovery, and action.

TechCrunch / 2026-10-06

Hark names privacy as the route into the AI assistant category

What happened: Hark released Hark Pro, an AI personal assistant positioned around using a computer end to end, while emphasizing privacy and task-related usefulness. The product exposes its web navigation to help users trust what the agent is doing. Why it matters: In a crowded assistant category, Hark is trying to make the name and promise do immediate classification work: private help that takes tasks off your plate. The positioning is less about abstract intelligence than a memorable relationship to the user's digital life. Watch: Watch whether visible actions and the privacy promise create enough recall and trust to overcome the category's growing sameness.

Meta expands the business assistant from answers to campaign action

What happened: Meta said Ads Creative Studio is expanding to more advertisers and that its Meta AI business assistant is gaining deeper business context plus capabilities to plan, create, and optimize campaigns and schedule tasks and alerts. Watch: Watch whether the assistant's broader promise stays anchored to concrete campaign jobs, or becomes a vague label for a bundle of unrelated automation.

Extreme weather turns a forecast into a merchandising constraint

What happened: Retail Dive reported that the strongest Super El Niño on record is expected to disrupt weather patterns, complicating inventory and merchandising decisions as brands plan for a warmer winter and uneven cold snaps. Why it matters: A broad external signal becomes useful only when translated into a specific decision frame: which seasonal promise will still feel true where the customer actually lives? Positioning has to meet lived conditions, not just calendar categories. Watch: Watch whether retailers shift from generic seasonal language to locally legible needs, assortments, and messages as weather makes the old category cues less reliable.

OpenAI treats text provenance as a trust label

What happened: OpenAI described its approach to text watermarking in response to EU AI Act requirements and explained a tool that can report whether it detects an OpenAI watermark without identifying the user or revealing prompts or conversations.
