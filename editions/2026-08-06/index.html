The Apprentice's Desk

The buyer is not a spreadsheet.

Rory Sutherland asks a more useful question than whether a choice is rational: what human logic made it useful? His answer turns research from a hunt for stated reasons into a search for the context, signals, and feelings that actually govern choice.

Apprenticeship Note

Do not mistake a customer's fluent explanation for the full cause of their behaviour. A useful account must explain what they do, not merely what they can say about it.

Chapter Reader

Read this compact section twice. First, identify the distinction Sutherland draws between optimisation and usefulness. Then read again for its working implication: a decision that looks irrational in an abstract model may make complete sense in its social setting.

01 / Name the operating system

Sutherland introduces psycho-logic to separate the way humans actually decide from the simplified logic of mathematics or introductory economics. The claim is not that reason is useless; it is that human judgement evolved to be useful in lived conditions, not to look optimal in a model.

Mark the move carefully. It gives you permission to inspect a behaviour before correcting it. A choice may carry information about safety, identity, effort, reciprocity, or context that a narrow calculation cannot see.

02 / Do not use emotion as a dismissal

The section resists the reflex to make emotion logic's inferior twin. Feelings are often treated as evidence of error because they are difficult to justify in a boardroom. Sutherland instead treats them as signals whose cause may be real even when the person cannot state it cleanly.

That distinction matters in commercial work. The task is not to flatter every stated preference or invent a manipulative trigger; it is to discover which human condition changes the meaning of the offer.

03 / Put context back into the explanation

Sutherland's examples show that the same material act can mean opposite things once the social setting changes. An incentive can be useful in one context and insulting in another. A model that removes the setting can therefore produce a precise answer to the wrong problem.

For a marketer, this is a research standard: do not ask only which claim wins. Ask what situation, relationship, and perceived trade-off make that claim feel credible, generous, risky, or beside the point.

Close Reading Sequence

  1. In your own words, distinguish a decision that is optimal in a model from one that is useful in a human setting. What information does the model leave out?
  2. Choose one customer behaviour your team has labelled irrational. List three contextual explanations before proposing a fix.
  3. Where could calling a buyer 'emotional' hide a legitimate concern about trust, status, effort, or social risk?
  4. Take one incentive in your product or offer. In what situation could it change from generous to awkward or insulting?
  5. Write one research question that would reveal the real condition behind a customer's stated reason, without leading them toward your preferred answer.

02 / The Principle

Useful beats tidy when people are making the choice.

A commercial explanation earns its keep when it accounts for behaviour in context. Do not reject an observation because it violates a clean model; use the violation to find the missing human variable.

One example

A free implementation call can look like an obvious conversion incentive. For a time-poor buyer who fears a hard sell, it may instead create a new obligation. Reframing it as a short fit check with a clear agenda can change the social meaning without changing the service.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one confusing customer behaviour: a stalled trial, a low-value plan choice, or an ignored feature. Make two columns: the stated reason and the possible psycho-logic. Add at least three contextual hypotheses, then write the smallest observation or test that could distinguish them.

03 / Field Notes

Three signals about context, memory, and visible jobs.

Marketing Dive / 2026-08-03

Goldfish makes back-to-school proof feel familiar

What happened: Goldfish is extending its long-running "The Snack That Smiles Back" platform for back-to-school with parent-facing messages about real cheese, no artificial flavors or colors, and baked rather than fried snacks. Why it matters: This is Sutherland's psycho-logic in ordinary grocery form: the rational claims matter because they are wrapped in a memory structure parents already trust. The campaign is not asking buyers to process a new category argument from scratch. Watch: Whether more packaged-food brands pair cleaner-label proof with old assets instead of treating nostalgia and functional reassurance as separate campaigns.

Express uses Y2K memory as a confidence shortcut

What happened: Express launched "Too Easy to Look This Good," a fashion-and-music campaign running across streaming video, digital, social, print, stores, and ecommerce while building on its "Dress to Express" platform. Why it matters: The strategic move is not simply nostalgia. It uses a remembered cultural texture to reduce the buyer's effort: confidence and self-expression become easier to feel before the product details are evaluated. Watch: Whether the campaign keeps one emotional cue intact across store, social, and product pages, or lets each channel fragment the promise.

Meta frames coding agents around bounded work, not vague magic

What happened: Meta released Muse Code in beta, positioning it as a terminal coding agent for large repositories that can plan changes, write code, validate results, and split large jobs across parallel sub-agents in isolated worktrees. Why it matters: The interesting commercial signal is the framing. Instead of selling general intelligence, the product is attached to a recognizable developer anxiety: complex codebase work without damaging the working copy. 05 / The Practice

Find the variable the model erased.

After reading, keep one behaviour that does not fit the tidy explanation. Your output is a short psycho-logic audit: the behaviour, the context you may be missing, and the smallest next observation that could reveal it.
