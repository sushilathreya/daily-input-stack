01 / Begin with the pattern, not the flattering customer story

Sharp's target is not the existence of repeat buying. It is the leap from repeat buying to a strategy built around a supposedly special, highly committed customer base.

Study the evidence before the interpretation. Ask what the data says about who buys the brand, how often they buy, and how much those buyers also buy from competitors.

02 / Duplication reveals shared markets

The duplication of purchase law describes a regular pattern: brands share buyers with other brands in proportion to those brands' size. Competitors are not isolated tribes; they draw from overlapping category buyers.

Notice how this changes the strategic question. Instead of searching for a narrow identity that explains every buyer, a marketer must make the brand easier for more category buyers to notice and choose.

03 / Loyalty metrics need a market context

A larger brand will often have more loyal buyers in absolute terms and somewhat stronger loyalty metrics, but that does not prove a fundamentally different kind of customer. Size and buying frequency can create the appearance of a special relationship.

Read for the difference between describing a brand's current customer mix and explaining how the brand could grow. The second claim requires evidence about reach, penetration, and shared buying.

04 / Turn the myth into a better growth audit

Take one brand whose plan is framed around retention, devotion, or community. Identify which part is observed behavior and which part is an unsupported theory about why the behavior exists.

Then ask what would change if growth meant becoming easier to buy for more light and occasional category buyers, while still serving existing customers well.

02 / The Principle

Do not let loyal buyers hide the reach problem.

Repeat buying is real, but it does not by itself prove that a brand grows through a special customer tribe. Use shared-buying patterns and penetration evidence to separate a description of current buyers from a credible growth strategy.

One example

A meal-kit company sees that its most frequent customers renew often and builds a plan around making them even more devoted. A broader audit may show that the company is simply under-reaching light category buyers, who also buy from competing services. The growth opportunity is to become easier to notice and try without abandoning existing customers.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one brand. Write its current repeat-buying evidence, the loyalty story your team tells about that evidence, and the missing penetration or shared-buying measure that could confirm or weaken the story.

Marketing Dive / 2026-10-26

Clorox turns brand marketing into a continuous relevance system

What happened: Clorox described replacing isolated campaign launches with systems that use people, AI automation, partners, social listening, and rapid testing across Pine-Sol, Brita, and Hidden Valley. Why it matters: The useful growth question is not whether a brand can make one memorable campaign; it is whether the organization can keep becoming more relevant to more category buyers while the opportunity is still live. Watch: Track whether faster signal-to-action improves reach and trial, not only engagement from the brand's existing audience.

Google makes the world model more steerable in real time

What happened: Google DeepMind released Genie 4 as a research preview with audio, more realistic interactive worlds, and the ability to change scenes through prompts while a simulation is running. Anthropic expands enterprise accounts across products

What happened: Anthropic's filing said more than half of customers spending at least $100,000 annually now use both its Claude platform and a first-party app such as Claude Code or Cowork, up from about 30% at the end of last year. Why it matters: This is a concrete example of expansion through broader category usage rather than a loyalty story alone: the account becomes more valuable when the product reaches additional jobs and teams. Etsy makes authenticity a visible part of product discovery

What happened: Etsy updated seller rules to require an authentic, non-AI-generated image in most listings, restrict AI-generated functional guides, and add tools to flag likely AI imagery while using AI internally for discovery and matching. Why it matters: The marketplace is making a category distinction legible at the moment of choice: AI can improve matching, but visible proof of human making protects the reason some buyers choose Etsy.
