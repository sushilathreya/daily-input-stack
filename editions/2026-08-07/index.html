The Apprentice's Desk

The evidence can be right and still point you wrong.

Clayton Christensen begins with a paradox: excellent companies can listen closely to customers, invest hard in better products, and still be organised to miss the next market. The mistake is not ignorance of technology; it is treating the current performance measure as the only one that counts.

Apprenticeship Note

A strong customer signal can be true and still be incomplete. Ask which job, segment, or performance dimension the signal cannot see before turning it into a roadmap rule.

01 / The Canon

Christensen: Better technology is not always better for the market that matters next.

Read the two-page section "The Impact of Technological Change" from Chapter 1 of Clayton M. Christensen's The Innovator's Dilemma. It separates changes that extend an established performance trajectory from changes that reset what performance means - and shows why the second kind can unseat well-managed incumbents.

Chapter Reader

Read once for the reversal: the pace and difficulty of change are not the cause of leaders' failure. Read again to identify the hidden measurement choice: which performance trajectory defines success, and whose use case does it leave out?

01 / Reject the comforting diagnosis

Christensen tests the intuitive story that leaders lose because technology moves too fast for them to keep up. His disk-drive evidence leads elsewhere: the established firms were not simply passive, arrogant, or unable to execute. The speed and difficulty of change did not explain the pattern.

That is the useful discipline in this opening. Do not accept a flattering or easy diagnosis merely because it explains a failure in familiar language. Locate the measurement that would distinguish lack of effort from a system that rewards the wrong response.

02 / Name the trajectory

Some technologies sustain the trajectory customers already value. They can be incremental or radical, but they make the existing product better by the established measure. In the disk-drive industry, incumbents routinely led these changes because their customers wanted more of the performance they already recognised.

A disruptive technology works differently. It changes or redefines the performance trajectory, often serving a different use case at first. Asking whether it is simply better than the current product misses the decision: better for whom, on which dimension, and at what stage of adoption?

03 / Treat the early mismatch as evidence

The section sets up the central tension: an entrant can initially look inferior on the incumbent's metric while being the beginning of a new trajectory. Leaders may then make locally sensible choices - investing in what current customers reward - while becoming less able to see the new market's logic.

For commercial work, this is a guardrail against both panic and complacency. Do not call every novelty disruptive. But when a smaller, simpler, or apparently weaker offer is winning a distinct job, investigate the new performance dimension before comparing feature lists.

Close Reading Sequence

  1. What evidence does Christensen use to reject the claim that leaders failed because technological change was simply too fast or difficult?
  2. In your own words, distinguish a sustaining technology from a disruptive one without using the words incremental, radical, better, or worse.
  3. Name one metric your team treats as the definition of product progress. Which customer or use case might that metric fail to represent?
  4. Find a product that looked weaker on the incumbent's main measure when it entered. What alternate performance trajectory made it useful to its first users?
  5. What observation would tell you that a new offer is merely a novelty rather than the start of a distinct performance trajectory?

02 / The Principle

A performance metric is a map, not the territory.

Customer evidence is indispensable for sustaining work, but it cannot reveal a market that has not yet learned how to ask. When a new offer underperforms on the established measure, inspect the job and trajectory before deciding it has no future.

One example

A design team keeps increasing an analytics product's number of dashboards because power users ask for more control. A lightweight daily briefing looks weak beside that suite. But if managers adopt it because it replaces a 20-minute morning scan with one clear decision, the comparison is not dashboard count; it is time-to-orientation.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one emerging competitor, workflow, or customer workaround. Make two columns: the established performance measure and the possible new trajectory. For each, write the user, the job, what initially looks worse, and the smallest interview or usage observation that could test whether the new trajectory is real.

03 / Field Notes

Three reminders that a new trajectory first looks like a weaker version of the old thing.

TechCrunch / 2026-08-07

Runway turns a crowded model market into a routing decision

What happened: Runway launched an AI model router as the market for generative-media models becomes more crowded, making model selection part of the product workflow rather than a one-time platform choice. Why it matters: The useful shift is from buying the highest headline capability to matching a model to a job, constraint, and moment. That is a different performance trajectory from treating one model as the product. Watch: Whether creative-AI products make routing legible as a user benefit, or leave customers to infer why a task was assigned to one model rather than another.

Google Photos adds a Video Remix tool

What happened: Google Photos added an AI Video Remix feature, moving a generative capability into a familiar personal-media workflow rather than asking users to begin inside a specialist creation tool. Why it matters: A capability can look less impressive beside a dedicated generator and still win on a new measure: how little setup it takes to turn an existing memory into something shareable. Watch: Whether consumer AI features earn repeat use through placement in existing habits instead of through increasingly elaborate creation controls.

Google's deepfake detector is used to test a political-image claim

What happened: TechCrunch reported that Google's deepfake-detection system was used to help debunk an allegedly manipulated image, giving a detection technology a concrete public-facing verification job. Why it matters: Trust technologies are often evaluated as abstract accuracy claims. Their real trajectory may be whether they fit into the moment someone needs a decision, explanation, and evidence trail. Watch: Whether provenance and detection tools compete on workflow clarity and usable proof, not only on technical benchmark results.
