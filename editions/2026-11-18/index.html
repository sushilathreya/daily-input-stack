01 / Name the association

Positioning begins by choosing the mental association a subject wants to make easiest to retrieve.

Mark the phrase or image that gives the place a memorable handle rather than a catalogue of attributes.

02 / Notice the tradeoff

A sharp position gains force by making some claims less central. Notice what the chosen frame excludes and why that sacrifice improves memorability.

Ask which competing interpretation the position is designed to displace.

03 / Connect frame to proof

The association has to be supported by details people can recognize, not merely repeated as a slogan.

Underline the evidence that makes the intended frame feel like a credible expectation.

04 / Audit one position

Choose one brand, destination, or product that currently tries to stand for too many things.

Rewrite its position as one association, one rejected frame, and one proof point.

02 / The Principle

Let one place stand for one idea.

A memorable position is a shortcut through a crowded category: one association, a clear contrast, and enough proof to make the association credible.

One example

A destination does not need to claim every attractive quality. It can own one travel expectation and let the name, itinerary, imagery, and proof reinforce that expectation.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Take one brand or place and write three lines: the association to own, the competing frame to reject, and the proof that should make the association credible.

03 / Field Notes

Five current signals on making a position clear enough to choose.

OpenAI / 2026-11-17

OpenAI gives sales teams one job-shaped starting point

What happened: OpenAI introduced ChatGPT for Sales, including a Sales plugin and more than 30 new or expanded partner plugins for account selection, call preparation, tailored proposals, follow-up, and pipeline risk. Why it matters: The offer is framed around a recognizable job—shortening the sales cycle—rather than asking buyers to remember a long list of AI capabilities. A narrow job makes the product easier to place in an existing workflow. Watch: When evaluating an AI product, ask whether its first screen names the job it owns and the next action it helps someone take, or merely repeats the category label.

Meta Newsroom / 2026-11-17

Meta puts a personal AI agent in a new market

What happened: Meta made Muse available in Australia and described it as a personal AI agent built for everyone, extending the app beyond a feature announcement into a distinct consumer proposition. Why it matters: The label does positioning work before the feature list begins: personal, agentic, and broadly accessible. The promise will be credible only if the first interactions make the user's own context and intent feel central. Watch: Track the first repeatable behavior Muse creates—creation, conversation, or task completion—and whether users can explain the difference from a general chatbot after one session.

Apple Newsroom / 2026-11-15

Apple tests whether health AI can earn trust through evidence

What happened: Apple launched a research study exploring how AI could help people understand health information and prepare for conversations with clinicians, using participant data and feedback to evaluate the experience. Why it matters: In a high-trust category, the position cannot be only 'AI for health.' The study frames the useful role more narrowly—as preparation and understanding—while evidence and clinical context supply the proof. Watch: Separate the promised association from the proof system behind it: who validates the output, what uncertainty is shown, and whether the product helps a person take a safer next step.

Shopify / 2026-11-13

Shopify says the commerce shelf is becoming an agentic recommendation layer

What happened: Shopify reported that AI-driven traffic to its stores grew 2.6x year over year and AI-referred orders grew 3.3x; it said about 75% of AI-referred purchases came from outside the top 100 product categories. Why it matters: Discovery is shifting from owning a visible shelf to being legible to the system making the recommendation. A product's use case, difference, and proof need to survive an agent's interpretation, not just a storefront visit. Watch: Audit whether product data states the intended occasion and fit clearly enough for an agent to recommend the item beyond the obvious category shortlist.

04 / Collected Fragments

One fragment from the timeline worth carrying into the work.

Palmer Luckey / following / 2026-11-18

A founder rejects the consensus frame for his next product

Luckey says SLAB is an older seastead project rather than the AI datacenter story attached to it. The fragment is useful as a positioning example: he is fighting to choose the frame people carry forward, even when the more current category would be easier to repeat.

Selling Oculus to Facebook in 2014 gave me money for helicopter flight lessons and developing a seastead called SLAB with a 1,000 year design life. The goal was to be cheaper than land and absent state/local laws that cripple critical American industries like metal processing, medical research/treatment, desalination, oil refining, chemical engineering, and more. Me and a few close friends cashed it up at a $1B valuation back in 2017, and while it still has a long way to go, there has been significant progress towards my goal of reviving manifest destiny and westward expansion. At a time when most of the seasteading community wanted to escape the US and smoke pot, this was the one project explicitly designed to make America stronger, a relief valve for ill considered or outdated rules and regulations. I am only talking about it now because a few media outlets got wind of me and some friends topping up the coffers and plan on calling SLAB my new floating AI datacenter project. It is way cooler than that, way older than that, and I hate looking like a hype-chasing consensus loser, so now you have to read this tweet.
