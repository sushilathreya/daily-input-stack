01 / A plan bridges the risk

Miller frames commitment as a crossing: customers want the outcome, but the perceived cost of choosing badly keeps them at the edge.

A plan places visible stepping stones between the customer's present problem and the desired result, making progress feel possible rather than perilous.

02 / Clarity prevents drift

The first job of a plan is to answer the customer's immediate question: what should I do now? Clear sequencing removes the confusion that can become an excuse not to act.

Study how a simple process can turn a vague promise into a path the customer can picture and follow.

03 / Process plans explain the relationship

A process plan clarifies how the customer does business with the company. It makes the exchange legible by showing what happens first, next, and after the purchase.

The plan is useful when it reduces cognitive load without burying the customer in operational detail.

04 / Agreement plans reduce perceived risk

An agreement plan names the commitments or expectations that protect both sides. It helps the customer understand what is included and what a successful engagement requires.

By addressing the fear beneath the decision, the plan makes trust practical: the customer can see how uncertainty will be contained.

05 / Turn the idea into one customer plan

Choose one offer and write three to five steps that move a customer from interest to a meaningful result. Remove any step that exists only because the company is organized that way.

Stop with one customer action plan. Every step should clarify the path, lower a specific risk, or make the next decision easier.

02 / The Principle

Turn uncertainty into a sequence.

Customers trust a guide who makes progress visible. Clarify how the relationship works, reduce the risk of commitment, and give the customer a small sequence they can believe in.

Example

A consulting offer might show three steps: diagnose the constraint, agree on one intervention, and review the result after two weeks. The sequence makes the work concrete before the buyer commits to a larger engagement.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one offer and write a three-step process plan plus one sentence that reduces the customer's main fear of buying. Cut every step that does not clarify progress or lower risk.

Rajiv Ayyangar / for_you / 2026-10-12

A market fragment on building a hull around an AI product

Rajiv Ayyangar argues that AI products need a durable hull—such as proprietary data, a vertical harness, network effects, or distribution—so they rise with model improvements instead of becoming redundant.

As CEO of Product Hunt, I see hundreds of launches a week. One trait separates winners from losers. Every few months, new models come out. Some products become better, effortlessly. Some don't improve, or become irrelevant. This property needs a name. I'm calling it Buoyancy. Buoyant startups rise with model improvements the way a boat rises with the tide. To be buoyant, you need a "hull" - usually proprietary data, a vertical-specific harness, network effects, or distribution advantage. This hull keeps you buoyant as models improve. Hulls are the new moats.

levelsio / for_you / 2026-10-12

A market fragment on the moats customers can actually feel

Levelsio lists proprietary content, community, distribution, physical operations, capital, and regulation as forms of defensibility beyond code alone.

I'd expand the moats left to: - Proprietary data or content: data nobody else has, or unique content you can create - Network effects / community: users make it more valuable for each other - Audience / distribution: you can get attention on demand - Physical component: hardware, locations, real-world operations - Capital: owning assets and investing (esp into the AI companies as they are and will suck up large parts of the economy) - Regulatory: licenses and legal barriers
