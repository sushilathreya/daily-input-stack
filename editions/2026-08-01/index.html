Chapter Reader

McKee's Chapter 7 is not the whole five-part story design. It is the foundation beneath it: story material becomes dramatic when a character acts with an expectation and reality answers differently.

01 / Substance Before Shape

Begin with McKee's title question: what is the substance from which story is made? The answer is not information, plot summary, or theme stated aloud. Story substance is human action under pressure, shaped by values that change.

For commercial narrative, this means an audience must feel a change in lived condition before it can care about a solution.

02 / Values In Motion

Track McKee's use of value. A scene matters because life moves from one charge to another: safety to danger, ignorance to knowledge, power to weakness, isolation to belonging. A neutral event does not carry story energy until it changes the value at stake.

When reading, write the value beside each example. The practical question is: what human value changes because of this action?

03 / The Gap

The core mechanism is the gap between expectation and result. A character acts expecting the world to respond in one way. The world responds differently. That split forces a new action, a new understanding, or a new level of risk.

This is the day's idea. Do not race forward into Inciting Incident, Act Design, Crisis, Climax, or Resolution. Stay with the smaller unit: action, expectation, unexpected result, changed value.

04 / Choice Under Pressure

McKee's gap matters because it exposes character. When reality resists the expected result, the character must choose what to do next. The audience learns through pressure, not explanation.

For a brand story, the equivalent is not “our customer had a problem.” It is: they took a reasonable action, the world answered differently, and the next choice revealed what was really at stake.

02 / The Principle

Don't tell us the benefit. Disturb the old normal.

People follow a narrative because their prediction of the world fails. A good opening creates a precise mismatch; the rest earns the correction.

One example

A founder's “we built an AI tool” is a category statement. “Our support queue doubled after the launch; the team could answer faster but could no longer tell which customer was about to churn” is an inciting incident. The tool enters only after the lost balance is visible.

Deliberate practice — 15 minutes

Choose one offer. Draw five boxes: old normal; inciting incident; first attempt that fails; costly choice; new normal. Use facts from one customer conversation. Keep it to 120 words. If the choice costs nothing, rewrite the middle.

@Hopeful_Rego / For You / 1 August 2026

Functional cola is a category with a longer memory than its current hype.

This earns its place because it pairs a specific product artifact with a category claim. Coca-Cola Plus gives the new “prebiotic soda” story some history: desire for cola has been re-routed through health, ingredients, and certification before. Useful question: which supposedly new category is really an old desire, given a fresh proof mechanism?

Long before today's prebiotic soda boom, Coca-Cola launched Coca-Cola Plus in Japan in 2017. A functional cola with added dietary fibre.

@rorysutherland / Following / 31 July 2026

The first diagnosis often controls the rest of the work.

Sutherland's “diagnostic momentum” is a sharp warning for marketers and product people. The initial frame can determine the options that get considered later. Before drafting the story, audit the diagnosis: is this really a retention problem, a trust problem, a status problem, or an onboarding problem?

There is a concept in medicine called “diagnostic momentum”. Whoever gets to the patient first will tend to diagnose them according to their own specialism, and then that first framing carries on down the line.
