01 / Recognition is not the same as position

A familiar name can make a new offer easier to notice, but attention does not tell the buyer what the offer stands for.

Study the difference between being remembered as part of a brand family and owning a distinct place in the prospect's mind.

02 / The parent meaning sets the comparison

When an extension carries the original name, the buyer brings the original category, expectations, and alternatives into the decision.

Notice whether those inherited associations clarify the new choice or force the extension to explain why it is different.

03 / One name can make two promises compete

The trap appears when the new offer needs a meaning that conflicts with the association the name already owns.

Choose one extension and write the position it inherited, the position it wants, and the evidence that the two can coexist without confusing the buyer.

04 / Protect the mental handle

The strategic question is not whether the extension can be sold once. It is whether the brand can remain easy to classify and recall after the portfolio expands.

Decide whether the offer needs a narrower promise, a clearer name, or a separate position.

02 / The Principle

A larger portfolio is not automatically a stronger position.

An extension can add revenue while subtracting clarity. Keep the name and promise aligned with the single useful association the buyer should carry forward.

One example

A premium workflow product adds a low-cost team version under the same name. If the name means high-touch enterprise control, the cheaper offer may need a sharper promise or a distinct handle rather than asking one association to carry both.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one extension. Write the parent brand's current position, the extension's intended position, the inherited comparison it triggers, and one change that would make the new offer easier to classify.

03 / Field Notes

Four current signals about how familiar systems borrow trust while protecting a clear position.

TechCrunch / 2026-10-23

Startups do not need virality to win customers

What happened: TechCrunch reports that startup operators at Disrupt described several routes to customers: Gamma used creator ambassadors across distinct segments, while Engine relied on events and direct sales rather than a visible social strategy. Why it matters: A channel can borrow attention without supplying a usable position. The strongest acquisition motion gives each audience a reason to understand the product, instead of treating reach as proof that the offer is legible. Watch: Watch whether creator programs preserve a segment-specific promise and repeatable conversion path, or merely increase exposure around a name buyers still cannot classify.

Etsy requires more visible proof of human making

What happened: Etsy updated its AI rules so most listings must include at least one authentic, unedited product image; AI-generated functional guides are disallowed, and the marketplace is adding detection and enforcement tools. Why it matters: Etsy is using product policy to defend the association that differentiates its marketplace: craftsmanship. Watch: Watch whether visible proof and enforcement restore buyer confidence without making the seller experience so burdensome that distinctive inventory leaves the marketplace.

Walmart gives shopping agents a bounded identity

What happened: Walmart announced Agent Profiles with purchase approvals or spending limits, plus an Agent Gateway using MCP so external systems can shop without receiving a customer’s payment information or purchase history. OpenAI moves frontier training away from the live web

05 / The Practice

Make the new offer legible before making the portfolio larger.

Use recognition as an entry point, but protect the distinct association that lets the buyer know why this offer deserves a place of its own.
