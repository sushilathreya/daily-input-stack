Give the disruptive bet to the customers who need it.

In Chapter 5 of The Innovator's Dilemma, Clayton Christensen explains why disruptive technologies need an organizational home whose customers value their trajectory. The team defending the established market is usually the wrong team to carry the new bet.

Apprenticeship Note

Chapter Reader

Read this chapter as a test of organizational fit. Mark each place where customer demand, margins, cost structure, or performance expectations make an established unit a poor owner for a disruptive project. Then identify the small market, autonomous team, or emerging customer whose needs would make the new trajectory rational before it becomes attractive to the core business.

01 / The wrong owner can make the right technology look irrational

Christensen begins with a practical consequence of disruption: a technology's prospects depend partly on which organization is asked to develop it. The unit built to serve today's best customers will evaluate the new trajectory against requirements those customers do not yet value.

This is not a talent problem. It is a fit problem between the project, the customer, and the resource-allocation logic used to judge progress.

02 / Let emerging customers pull the project forward

A disruptive technology needs customers who experience its initial trade-offs as acceptable or useful. Their needs create a market in which the technology can improve without being rejected for failing the incumbent market's performance standard.

The early customer is therefore more than a buyer. It is the context that gives the technology time, feedback, and a reason to exist.

03 / Autonomy protects a different resource logic

When a disruptive effort remains subordinate to the established business, its priorities are repeatedly compared with larger, more profitable, and more urgent programs. A separate organization can use different measures of progress and make decisions at the scale of the emerging market.

Autonomy is useful when it preserves accountability to a real customer while reducing the core business's power to veto every unfamiliar move.

04 / The organization follows its value network

The chapter connects organizational behavior to value networks. Managers allocate resources toward the customers and economics that sustain the unit, so the organization naturally becomes good at the trajectory its network rewards.

A disruptive project needs a network that values its present performance, not a promise that it will immediately satisfy the standards of a mature one.

05 / Assign the bet where the need is already visible

The practical decision is to locate the customers for whom the new technology's current limitations are outweighed by a different benefit. Give responsibility to the organization that can hear those customers and survive the early economics.

Stop with an ownership audit: the disruptive project, its first customers, the metric those customers value, the unit currently responsible, and the structural reason that unit may resist the work.

02 / The Principle

Put the disruptive bet where its first customers can reward it.

A new technology is often rejected because it is measured inside the value network that does not yet need it. Assign the work to a team with customers, economics, and incentives that make the emerging trajectory worth pursuing now.

Example

An enterprise software company should not ask its largest-account team to lead a low-cost self-serve product whose first users value speed and simplicity over integration depth. A small team serving those users can learn the new model before the core business has reason to defend it.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Pick one disruptive initiative. Name the first customers who need it, the result they value, the unit currently owning it, and the unit that would be better aligned. Finish by stating which incumbent metric should not decide the project's fate.

03 / Field Notes

Five current signals on putting new capability where the first users can reward it.

TechCrunch / 2026-09-17

PrismML bets that useful reasoning can live on the device

What happened: PrismML released Bonsai 2 27B, a compressed reasoning model reduced to 5.9 GB, with the stated aim of making capable models practical on PCs and possibly high-end phones. Why it matters: This is a classic disruptive-entry shape: the early customer is not the data-center buyer demanding maximum scale, but the person who values local access, privacy, and low latency enough to accept a different performance-cost trade-off. The product gets a market in which its current constraint is part of the appeal. Watch: Watch whether on-device users create a durable value network for compressed models, or whether cloud quality remains the only performance standard that matters.

Retail Dive / 2026-09-16

Lowe's puts digital commerce inside the chief marketer's remit

What happened: Lowe's promoted CMO Jen Wilson to EVP and added Lowes.com and Marketplace to a portfolio already covering marketing, retail media, loyalty, and personalization; online sales were up 15.7% in Q2 2026. Why it matters: The move places the promise, the discovery surface, and the transaction path closer together. It is an operating response to a customer journey that no longer fits neatly inside separate brand, commerce, and retention departments. Watch: Watch whether the expanded remit produces a more coherent customer experience or simply concentrates disconnected channel metrics under one executive.

Marketing Dive / 2026-09-16

Sonos trades specification-led positioning for experience

What happened: Sonos is refreshing its brand strategy around emotions and experiences rather than primarily appealing to audiophiles as it works to recover from a prior product misstep. Why it matters: A repositioning only matters when it gives the organization a customer it can serve differently. Moving from technical specifications toward lived experience is a bet that a broader audience values the role sound plays in life more than it values a spec comparison. Watch: Watch whether product reliability, retail presentation, and support behavior reinforce the new promise; a campaign cannot create a new value network by itself.

Retail Dive / 2026-09-14

Teen shoppers are giving AI recommendation authority

What happened: A Mastercard survey of 26,000 parents and teens found that 31% of teenagers trust AI-generated product recommendations over a friend's, 23% over a parent's, and 27% are likely to use a fully AI-run shopping assistant. Why it matters: The emerging customer for AI commerce may not be the shopper who wants a better search box, but the shopper willing to delegate judgment. That creates a different performance standard: useful context, confidence, and completion matter more than a larger catalogue alone. Watch: Watch whether delegated recommendations increase conversion and satisfaction, or whether trust falls when the assistant's rationale, bias, or errors become visible.

TechCrunch / 2026-09-17

AI agents are moving into the phone-call layer

What happened: Instinct and Meta's Muse both added or expanded outbound calling, aimed at tasks such as booking restaurants, joining cancellation lists, and handling service problems; the features are being shaped partly by user requests. Why it matters: The early market is defined by jobs that mainstream software has left awkward or impossible, such as calling a business that has no online booking. Those customers can reward an imperfect agent because the alternative is still friction, giving the new capability room to improve. Watch: Watch which narrow, high-friction jobs become repeatable enough to sustain agent adoption before broader assistant promises become credible.
