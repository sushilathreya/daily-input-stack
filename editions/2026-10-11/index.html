Chapter Reader

Read this as a study of useful credibility. Mark where Miller keeps the customer in the hero's role, how empathy creates recognition, and how authority creates confidence without turning the brand into the center of the story. Then write one guide-positioning brief for an offer you know.

01 / The customer is the hero

Miller's central correction is positional: the customer faces the conflict and wants to win the day, while the brand should help the customer do so.

A brand that casts itself as the hero asks the audience to admire the wrong character. The guide exists to equip the person whose problem actually matters.

02 / A guide understands the struggle

Empathy tells the customer that the brand recognizes the internal problem beneath the visible task. It makes the offer feel relevant before it makes the offer impressive.

The chapter treats empathy as a clear statement of shared understanding, not as a sentimental slogan detached from the customer's real frustration.

03 / Authority creates confidence

Authority is competence made legible. Testimonials, statistics, awards, and customer logos let people verify that the guide has helped others with a similar challenge.

The proof should answer the customer's quiet question—can this brand help me?—without making the brand's accomplishments the main story.

04 / Empathy and authority work together

Empathy without competence may feel warm but cannot move the story forward. Authority without empathy can feel distant, boastful, or irrelevant.

The guide's one-two punch is to show both that it understands the customer's situation and that it knows how to help the customer make progress.

05 / Turn credibility into a guide brief

Choose one offer and name the customer's internal problem, the empathetic statement that would make the customer feel seen, and the smallest proof of competence that would create confidence.

Stop with one guide-positioning brief. Keep the customer at the center and make the brand's role specific: understand the struggle, show the capability, and invite the next step.

02 / The Principle

Earn trust by helping the customer win.

A brand becomes credible when it understands the customer's difficulty and demonstrates the competence to help. Keep the customer in the hero's role; use empathy to create recognition and authority to create confidence.

Example

A payroll service might begin by acknowledging the anxiety of getting a small team's pay right, then show a short customer testimonial and a clear accuracy statistic. The customer remains the protagonist; the service proves it can guide the work.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one offer and write three lines: the customer's internal problem, an empathetic statement that shows you understand it, and one proof point that demonstrates competence. Remove any sentence that makes the brand the hero.

03 / Field Notes

Four current signals on how brands become useful guides by making trust, proof, and participation visible.

Marketing Dive / 2026-10-08

Omaha Steaks makes quality mean something customers can verify

What happened: Omaha Steaks' No Bull platform replaces vague quality language with specific claims about U.S. sourcing, aging, and hand-cutting; the brand reported a 46% conversion-rate increase and 130% rise in social mentions about two weeks after launch. Why it matters: The campaign treats credibility as a customer service: it answers the shopper's uncertainty before asking for the order. Authority becomes useful when the claim is concrete enough for people to understand and assess. Watch: Watch whether the conversion lift holds beyond launch attention and whether different customer groups need different forms of proof.

Retail Dive / 2026-10-09

Prime Big Deal Days rewarded shoppers for finding the clearest value

What happened: Adobe data cited by Retail Dive put U.S. online sales during Amazon's two-day Prime Big Deal Days at $9.86 billion; mobile sales grew 12.8% year over year to $5.1 billion and made up 52% of online sales. Why it matters: In a crowded promotion, the guide's job is not merely to announce a discount but to reduce decision friction. Shoppers prioritized value, so the retailer that explains the tradeoff most clearly earns confidence as well as the transaction. Watch: Watch post-event repeat purchase and November demand to separate durable trust from a short-lived bargain response.

Marketing Dive / 2026-10-09

Free People let the collaborator carry part of the meaning

What happened: Free People and musician Willow co-created a fall campaign, live performance video, and record-release event, with the brand deliberately ceding creative control rather than treating the artist as a conventional ambassador. Why it matters: The brand acts as a facilitator of someone else's relationship with a community. That is guide behavior: provide resources and a credible platform while allowing the person with the lived cultural knowledge to remain central. Watch: Watch whether the relationship continues beyond a single campaign and whether the new audience recognizes the collaboration as reciprocal rather than extractive.

TechCrunch / 2026-10-09

AI infrastructure companies are using disclosure to repair trust

What happened: TechCrunch reported that Amazon will stop using NDAs when negotiating data-center deals with local governments, following Microsoft's similar move, after secrecy contributed to community backlash and moratoriums. Why it matters: Transparency is not the same as trust, but it can make trust possible by giving affected people enough information to judge the tradeoff. The guide earns a hearing by acknowledging the audience's concern instead of asking for admiration first. Watch: Watch whether disclosure changes community decisions or only changes the companies' explanation of them; proof has to affect the customer's risk, not just the message.

Patrick Collison / following / 2026-10-11

Personal agents may make product quality easier to discover

Patrick Collison argues that personal agents could reduce the advantage of short-attention demand routing and make product quality, fit, and evidence more important in purchase decisions.

Assorted things I've been mulling over regarding agents in the economy: • Companies are in some sense the original superintelligence. It's often the case that they can use their amortized cognitive surplus to advance their interests against those of certain consumers: relative to the optimization capacity of companies, consumers can be inattentive, underinformed, irrational, etc. • In corporate strategy, people have long debated the relative importance of distribution and product quality. Perhaps you've made something better, but does anyone know about it? Won't people just continue to buy the ACME Corp Mousetraps? Personal agents will, I think, act as a kind of structural subsidy for product quality. • Many internet companies have monetized demand routing, which is in turn often predicated on short attention spans. How will the nature and value of this routing change in a world of personal agents? I don't know the answer, but I think that the effects might be pretty big.

05 / The Practice

Make trust visible before asking for action.

Name the customer's struggle, express understanding, and provide just enough evidence of competence for the customer to believe the next step is safe.
