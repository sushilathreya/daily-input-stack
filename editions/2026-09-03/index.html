Chapter Reader

Read these two pages as a warning about analytical temperament. Sutherland is not attacking expertise itself; he is attacking the kind of expertise that trusts a neat model more than stubborn human reality. Mark where he implies that theory becomes brittle when it excludes context, tacit knowledge, emotion, or social meaning, and where he suggests that institutions do real damage when they optimize the wrong abstraction with too much confidence.

01 / The target is not intelligence but overconfidence in stripped-down explanation

Sutherland's criticism lands on a specific failure mode: smart people who become so attached to a clean theory that they stop noticing what ordinary behaviour is trying to tell them. The danger is not rigor by itself. The danger is confusing a model's internal consistency with a sufficient account of reality.

That distinction matters because technocratic thinking often sounds persuasive precisely when it has removed the inconvenient variables. Once human motives are flattened into what can be counted neatly, the explanation becomes easier to defend and less able to describe what people actually do.

02 / Observed irrationality may be evidence that the system is incomplete

This section extends the earlier Alchemy argument that weird behaviour deserves explanatory respect. If people keep choosing in ways that seem inefficient, embarrassing, or hard to justify, the first question is whether the theory has omitted status, reassurance, identity, ceremony, or some other human payoff.

Sutherland's warning is practical for commercial work. A team that rushes to correct the user may be correcting away the very signal that would have revealed what the offer, interface, or message still fails to honor.

03 / Institutions become more dangerous when their abstractions are enforced at scale

The section matters beyond individual judgment because technocratic errors do not stay private. Once an elegant simplification is embedded in policy, process, or product design, it can impose the wrong logic on large groups of people while still looking rational from inside the system.

Read this as a caution against optimization without anthropology. Better decisions start by asking what the abstraction leaves out, who bears the cost of that omission, and what recurring behaviour keeps pointing back to the missing variable.

Close Reading Sequence

  1. What kind of intelligence is Sutherland criticizing here: lack of expertise, or expertise that mistakes simplification for full explanation?
  2. Where in your work do people most quickly label behaviour irrational instead of asking what the model left out?
  3. What human variable would be easiest for a technocratic system to erase in your field: status, reassurance, effort, ritual, dignity, or something else?
  4. Choose one policy, product flow, or message you know well. What recurring user behaviour suggests the official explanation is incomplete?
  5. How would you test whether the system is wrong before concluding that the people are wrong?

02 / The Principle

When behaviour and theory diverge, protect the behaviour as evidence first.

A strong model should explain what people persistently do, not merely what planners wish were rational. Before optimizing around a clean abstraction, inspect which human motive, context, or social meaning the abstraction may have erased.

Example

A product team removes a concierge onboarding call because the self-serve flow is faster and cheaper. Activation drops among high-value accounts because the call was not just instruction; it was reassurance that a serious person had understood the buyer's stakes. The efficient model was neat, but it had priced the wrong thing.

Practice

Take one user behaviour your team treats as frustrating or irrational. Write the official explanation, then list three human variables the model may have omitted. Your output is the smallest test or observation that could tell you whether the behaviour is protecting one of those variables.

Five current signals on how platforms, brands, and retailers are trying to respect actual user behavior by moving closer to trust, context, and where action already starts.

Marketing Dive / 2026-08-27

Dr Pepper is keeping Fansville effective by treating creative variation as an operating system, not a one-off campaign embellishment

What happened: Marketing Dive reported on August 27 that Dr Pepper's ninth "Fansville" season is leaning on heavy personalization, with the brand saying last year's program included more than 2,500 marketing permutations that drove more than double the sales lift and about 30% higher incremental return on ad spend than its national campaign average. Why it matters: This is a brand-and-consumer signal because the campaign is succeeding by respecting how fragmented fandom and attention already are. Instead of pushing one polished national message, the brand is adapting the same world into many versions that meet different subcultures where they already care. Watch: Whether more large brands treat campaign systems as modular content engines with measurable variant performance instead of judging success mainly by one hero spot.
