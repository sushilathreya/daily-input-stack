Apprenticeship Note

A sample is not a giveaway added after the argument. It is a controlled way to let the product create belief, capture interest, and reveal the next step.

Hopkins: a sample turns a claim into a testable experience.

Chapter Reader

Read this chapter as a design brief for reducing belief friction. Mark where Hopkins distinguishes a sample from a mere gift, how the offer captures a next action, and which costs he insists must be judged per customer rather than per impression.

01 / Let the product demonstrate itself

Hopkins begins with the product as its own best salesman, surrounded by the mental impression and atmosphere the advertiser creates around it.

A sample is powerful because it lets the prospect encounter the claimed quality instead of asking an advertisement to carry the entire burden of belief.

02 / A sample creates a next action

The sample offer can make the word "Free" useful, prompt a coupon response, and give the advertiser the name and address of an interested prospect.

The important movement is from passive reading to an observable action that permits fuller information, follow-up, or product use.

03 / Remove needless friction

Hopkins argues that charging for a sample or making the prospect pay for the seller's effort can sharply reduce replies before conversion has occurred.

His advice is not to give blindly. It is to measure the cost of requests and customers, then test whether free sampling lowers the cost of gaining a buyer.

04 / Context determines the economics

Samples can appear expensive beside advertising, but Hopkins says the relevant measure is cost per customer. A sample can be the cheapest route when it produces qualified action and repeat use.

The final pages connect sampling to distribution and follow-up: the offer must create a practical path to supply, freshness of interest, and a purchase.

Close Reading Sequence

  1. What does Hopkins mean by the product's mental impression and atmosphere, and how does a sample help create it?
  2. Which parts of the sample offer turn a reader into an identifiable prospect rather than a temporary audience member?
  3. Where does Hopkins distinguish useful measurement from penny-wise resistance to sampling?
  4. What friction in one of your current offers could a well-designed trial or demonstration remove?
  5. What should you measure per customer to decide whether the sample is actually economical?

02 / The Principle

Design the proof so the prospect can take the next step.

A sample works when it lets the product support the promise and gives an interested prospect an easy, measurable next action. Judge it by qualified customers gained, not by the apparent cheapness of withholding it.

One example

Before asking a team to buy a workflow tool, offer a constrained trial that lets them complete one real job, captures the setup details needed for follow-up, and points directly to the plan that fits continued use.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one offer you are trying to grow. Design its smallest credible sample or trial, remove one unnecessary payment or response step, and write the customer-level metric that will tell you whether the test paid for itself.

03 / Field Notes

Four current signals on making proof tangible, contextual, and easy to act on.

OpenAI / 2026-09-10

OpenAI puts governed business data inside a conversational workflow

What happened: OpenAI introduced a Data agent in ChatGPT Work that connects to approved business data, respects existing permissions, uses organizational definitions, and turns questions into evidence-backed dashboards and approved actions. Why it matters: The product is a live demonstration of value: trusted context lets more people test a business question themselves, inspect the evidence, and move toward action without waiting for a report. The sample is not a generic demo; it is the user's own governed data. Watch: Whether teams return for consequential decisions and validate the findings, rather than treating the dashboards as impressive one-off experiments.

Marketing Dive / 2026-09-11

Cadbury makes generosity physical in a fashion-week activation

What happened: Cadbury's largest U.S. campaign pairs a New York Fashion Week collaboration with Luar and Nylon, an insulated crystal-studded clutch sized for shareable chocolate, a hero film, an episodic social series, and a sweepstakes for consumers. Why it matters: The campaign gives an abstract brand idea—generosity—a physical object and a path to participation. The proof is designed to be seen, shared, and entered, so the audience can experience the promise instead of only hearing it. Watch: Whether the spectacle creates qualified participation and brand lift, or remains a beautiful object whose attention does not travel into chocolate purchase or sharing behavior.

Instacart moves grocery discovery from search into a ready-to-buy cart
