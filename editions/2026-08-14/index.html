The best-run company can still miss the market that does not yet pay it to listen.

Clayton Christensen's next Chapter 1 section explains why strong incumbent behaviour can produce blindness rather than safety. Firms usually improve along the performance path their best customers reward; the dilemma begins when a new trajectory matters first to a smaller market the existing business is built to ignore.

Apprenticeship Note

Customer focus is not a universal defense. Ask which customers your current economics train you to hear clearly and which emerging job or segment that same discipline teaches you to dismiss.

01 / The Canon

Christensen: the same discipline that serves today's best market can hide tomorrow's viable one.

Read the Chapter 1 section "Sustaining Technological Changes" from Clayton M. Christensen's The Innovator's Dilemma, through the end of Chapter 1. Christensen shows that leading firms are usually very good at sustaining innovation because their customers, cost structures, and performance systems all reward that work. The danger is that these same strengths can make low-end or emerging trajectories look irrational until they have already become serious.

Chapter Reader

Read for the mechanism rather than the slogan. Christensen is not saying incumbents fail because they are lazy or anti-innovation. He is showing how rational allocation, customer listening, and demand for margins can produce choices that are locally sound and strategically fatal. Mark each place where a management virtue becomes a constraint once the relevant market shifts.

01 / Sustaining innovation usually fits the incumbent's system

Christensen's first move is to normalize incumbent success. Established firms often lead sustaining changes because those changes improve the dimensions their best customers already value. Their engineering talent, sales relationships, planning routines, and capital discipline all line up behind that work.

This matters because it blocks a shallow explanation. If leaders repeatedly win on sustaining improvements, the eventual failure cannot be reduced to incompetence. The more useful question is what kind of opportunity would fail to look attractive inside an organisation built to serve demanding, profitable customers well.

02 / Good management can filter out the wrong market too early

The section becomes sharper when Christensen ties innovation choice to market size and customer demand. A disruptive trajectory often begins in a smaller or less profitable segment, with buyers who value a different mix of attributes. To a well-managed incumbent, that looks like weak demand, weak margins, and weak use cases.

That is why the dilemma is structural. Managers are not ignoring evidence; they are obeying the evidence their current business is designed to treat as credible. The same filters that protect returns in the core can make a new market appear too small to deserve serious commitment.

03 / Resource dependence means the customer shapes what the firm can pursue

Christensen pushes past the heroic-manager myth by showing how firms depend on customers and investors for resources. The most powerful customers influence which performance improvements get prioritized, which products feel worth building, and which opportunities seem responsible enough to fund.

This shifts the strategic question. Instead of asking whether leadership has enough foresight, ask which external demands the company cannot easily refuse. A firm may understand the new trajectory in theory and still be unable to justify it within the economics and expectations of the current business.

04 / The early weakness of a disruptive offer is part of the pattern, not evidence against it

A disruptive technology rarely enters by beating the incumbent on the incumbent's favorite measure. It often looks smaller, slower, simpler, or less capable to the mainstream buyer. Christensen's argument is that this apparent weakness is exactly why leaders deprioritize it until the alternative performance path improves enough to invade the core.

For product and market work, this is the diagnostic test worth carrying forward. When a new offer looks inferior, separate 'inferior on the current metric' from 'incapable of anchoring a different job.' That distinction determines whether the newcomer is noise, a niche, or the front edge of a new curve.

05 / Strategy requires a place where the new trajectory can make sense on its own terms

By the end of the chapter, Christensen has prepared the reader for why disruptive work often needs a different organisational home, cost logic, or customer set. A mature core business is optimized to reject opportunities that are too small, too low-margin, or too misaligned with what current customers request.

The practical lesson is not to stop serving the core. It is to notice when the core's decision rules are being mistaken for universal truth. A new trajectory usually needs protection long enough to be judged by the job it serves, not by the standards of the business it may later unsettle.

02 / The Principle

Do not treat the core business's filters as a complete map of future demand.

A company earns the right to scale by listening hard to current customers and allocating resources carefully. But those same strengths can blind it to a new market whose first buyers want a different bargain. Before rejecting an emerging offer, ask whether it is weak in absolute terms or merely weak by the standards of the incumbent's present economics.

One example

A software company sells a robust enterprise analytics suite with long onboarding, high contract value, and deep admin controls. A lightweight self-serve tool aimed at individual operators looks trivial beside it: smaller deal size, fewer features, less customization. The incumbent keeps improving enterprise depth because its largest accounts keep rewarding that path. Meanwhile the simpler tool becomes the default for a growing class of teams that need speed and independence more than administrative power.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Choose one emerging competitor, workaround, or low-end product in your market. Write the incumbent metric that makes it look weak, the distinct job it may serve, and the business rule inside your company that would prevent you from backing it early. Then describe one way to test it without forcing it through the core's normal thresholds.

Five signals about how major platforms are redrawing the rules of visibility, control, and monetization as the next workflow shifts.

OpenAI / 2026-08-12

Frontier firms are moving AI from assistance into delegated work

What happened: OpenAI published two enterprise adoption reports saying frontier firms now generate 8.3 times as many output tokens per active user as typical firms, while Codex produced 64% of combined Codex and ChatGPT enterprise output tokens as agentic use spread beyond engineering into legal, sales, recruiting, and marketing. Why it matters: The gap is starting to come less from model access and more from whether a company has built the workflows, permissions, and habits that let agents do real work. That is exactly the kind of trajectory shift incumbents can underrate while they keep evaluating AI as a helper instead of a new operating layer. Watch: Whether more large firms push agentic workflows into non-engineering teams and standardize Plugins, skills, and human-review steps instead of leaving AI adoption at the pilot stage.
