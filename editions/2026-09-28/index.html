A follower wins by owning the alternative the leader cannot be.

In Chapter 7 of Positioning, Al Ries and Jack Trout explain how a follower can become memorable by choosing a credible alternative position instead of imitating the leader. The discipline is to make the difference easy to name and hard to confuse.

01 / The leader sets the reference point

Ries and Trout begin with the reality that a follower is rarely judged in a blank space. The leader has already established a category reference point, so the follower must decide how to relate to that existing memory.

This makes positioning a choice about the prospect's mind, not a list of everything the company can do. The first question is what comparison the customer will make automatically.

02 / Imitation gives the leader the advantage

A follower that copies the leader's claims invites the audience to ask why it should choose the less familiar version. Similarity may make the offer legible, but it does not give the mind a reason to store it separately.

The follower needs a deliberate contrast: a dimension, audience, use case, or experience that the leader does not own in the same way.

03 / A second position must be credible

Difference alone is not a position. A follower's alternative has to connect to something the organization can deliver and the customer can recognize as valuable.

The strongest contrast is often adjacent to the leader's strength but not a direct contest on identical terms. It gives the prospect a simple reason to choose differently.

04 / Memory rewards a narrow contrast

A follower becomes easier to remember when its distinction can be expressed without a paragraph of explanation. Narrowness is not a weakness here; it is what lets the contrast survive the noise of the category.

The test is whether a customer can finish the sentence: if the leader is the choice for one thing, this brand is the choice for what?

02 / The Principle

Choose the contrast the leader leaves open.

A follower earns memory by giving the customer a clear alternative to the leader, then making that alternative credible through what it actually delivers.

Example

A small project-management tool should not claim to be a smaller version of the category leader. It might instead own the position of the simplest workspace for a two-person team that needs decisions and deadlines visible without enterprise administration.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Name the leader in one category, list the position it owns, and write five alternatives your offer could credibly occupy. Choose the one that is easiest to prove and easiest for a customer to repeat.

03 / Field Notes

Four current signals on choosing a position people can recognize and trust.

Meta Newsroom / 2026-09-24

Meta turns its agent into a portable shopping position

What happened: At Connect 2026, Meta announced that its Muse personal AI agent is coming to AI glasses, can act on what a wearer is looking at, and is adding commerce connectors including Walmart, Sephora, Ulta, Shop Pay, and PayPal. Why it matters: Meta is defining a distinct position for ambient shopping help: the agent can interpret the product or list in front of you and act across connected merchants. The remembered difference is not just intelligence, but access at the moment of physical discovery. Watch: Watch whether hands-free context produces a useful alternative to search and apps, or merely adds another branded layer to an already crowded path to purchase.

Marketing Dive / 2026-09-25

Jeep turns one brand truth into several cultural entry points

What happened: Jeep launched “This Is Jeep Country,” linking its 85-year history to American heritage while adding culturally grounded executions for Black and Hispanic audiences across digital and online channels. Why it matters: A clear position can stay stable while the invitation into it changes. Jeep is trying to make one organizing idea recognizable without pretending every audience reaches that idea through the same cultural reference point. Watch: Watch whether the extensions feel like credible routes into the same brand or like translations that leave the underlying position unchanged and distant.

Retail Dive / 2026-09-24

Retailers are adopting agents faster than they can govern them

What happened: Retail Dive reported that retail employees’ use of ungoverned AI tools fell from 70% to 44%, while approved-tool use rose from 40% to 73%; Netskope also observed a 400% increase in retail AI agents interacting with remote MCP servers. Why it matters: In a crowded AI category, governance is becoming part of the position a vendor must own. “More capable” is not enough if buyers cannot explain where data goes, which systems an agent touches, or who remains accountable. Watch: Watch which products make control and visibility a simple, repeatable promise rather than a technical appendix added after the sale.

Retail Dive / 2026-09-23

Shoppers use AI recommendations without fully trusting them

What happened: A SmartCustomer survey reported that 76% of nearly 1,200 U.S. consumers had used AI to make purchases in the past year, while 89% did not fully trust AI recommendations and one-third regretted a purchase based on one. Why it matters: Adoption does not mean a position has been earned. Recommendation products need a visible reason to believe—source quality, retailer legitimacy, or human review—because the customer is already separating convenience from confidence. Watch: Watch whether AI commerce brands compete on better answers alone or make verification an explicit part of the product promise.

One fragment from the timeline worth carrying into the work.

Josh Elman / for_you / 2026-09-27

A market fragment on beating copycats through speed and supply

This is worth keeping because it reframes imitation as market evidence rather than only a legal threat. The useful lesson is that demand has to be met with speed, volume, and a stronger operating rhythm, not just protected by complaint after the opportunity is visible.

End the Blank Box Problem Consumer AI is trying to go mainstream. But it’s still too hard to learn. We’re trying to onboard hundreds of millions of people to one of the most powerful technologies ever built with an empty text field. Open almost any AI product and you know the screen. A blank box. Maybe a few example prompts underneath. The message is simple. Ask anything. The problem is you have to know what to ask. Some products go one step further and start by asking you to connect your life. Email. Calendar. Social accounts. Finances. Health. That may eventually make the product much more useful. But it is a huge request for trust before the user understands what they are going to get in return. For experienced AI users, none of this is much of a problem. You arrive with something you need done and the experience can feel magical. I couldn’t figure out a domain redirect. I handed the account to an agent and it fixed everything. My daughter needed sheet music converted so that repeats and codas became one continuous stream she could play. My Muse, Alfalfa, did it in two shots. Something that used to take me 45 minutes of PDF editing was suddenly easy. But most people don’t arrive with a backlog of jobs for an AI agent. They arrive curious. They want to understand what this thing can actually do for them. And a blank box teaches them almost nothing. So they try the examples they’ve heard about. Book a restaurant. Find cheaper insurance. Cancel a subscription. Those demos are impressive. They also have a hidden problem. You barely do them. You might book a special dinner a few times a year. Shop for insurance even less often. Once you cancel the subscription, the job is finished. These use cases are memorable because they show off what AI can do. They rarely create a reason to come back tomorrow. Every product has a purpose, a set of core actions, and a cycle. The cycle is how often those actions naturally repeat. A lot of the famous consumer AI demos have very long cycles. That makes it hard for a habit to form. It also slows down something even more important. Learning. The missing piece in consumer AI is discovery. People need to learn what AI can do by watching what other people figured out. Consumer products learned this decades ago. TikTok didn’t require you to arrive knowing what kind of video to make. You watched other people. You saw a format. You copied it. You changed it. New behaviors spread from person to person. The feed was also an education system for the product. AI barely has an equivalent. You sit alone with your blank box. You don’t see what someone like you figured out yesterday. You don’t stumble across a clever workflow and think, “I could use that.” You don’t get better simply by opening the product every day. We built extraordinarily capable products without building the systems that teach people what those capabilities are for. There is an obvious complication. A lot of the best AI use cases are private. That doesn’t mean the learning has to stay private. You don’t need my daughter’s sheet music to learn from what I did. You only need the recipe. “Turn repeats and codas into one continuous score.” The result stays private. The idea travels. That may be the missing consumer AI primitive. A feed of things people figured out how to do. Recipes you can copy, adapt, and run on your own information. Every successful recipe teaches you one more thing the product can do. And unlike a tutorial, the library keeps getting better because the users are discovering the product for each other. Maybe the next hundred million people don’t need better prompts. They need to see what the first hundred million figured out.
