Give the position a name the mind can keep.

In Chapter 10 of Positioning, Al Ries and Jack Trout show how a generic or borrowed name leaves an offer without a distinct place in the prospect's mind—and why naming is part of the positioning work.

Apprenticeship Note

A name is a mental handle. If it sounds interchangeable, borrowed, or too close to a competitor, the offer must spend its scarce attention explaining where it belongs.

Chapter Reader

01 / A no-name offer has no clear slot

The no-name trap begins when an offer sounds like a category description rather than a distinct choice. The prospect may understand the words while retaining no useful memory of the brand.

Study the difference between being legible and being ownable: a name can explain almost nothing, or it can give the intended position a handle.

02 / Generic language invites comparison on the leader's terms

When a brand uses the same broad language as the category leader, it often enters a comparison it did not choose. The larger or earlier name keeps the stronger association.

Notice how naming affects the frame before the buyer evaluates features, proof, or price.

03 / Borrowed meaning can blur the offer

A name that leans on an established association may gain instant familiarity, but it can also make the offer seem derivative or difficult to separate from the original.

The useful question is not whether a word is familiar. It is whether the familiarity points to the position this offer can credibly occupy.

04 / Distinctiveness is a strategic constraint

A sharper name may narrow the territory an offer can claim later. That is a real constraint, but a vague name creates a more immediate cost: the market has nowhere definite to place the offer now.

Read the tradeoff as a positioning decision. Choose the mental territory worth owning before optimizing for maximum future flexibility.

05 / Turn the name into a position brief

Choose one offer and write the category it wants to enter, the position it wants to own, and the name or verbal handle that makes that position easier to remember.

Stop with one name-and-position brief. Remove any cleverness that does not help the prospect classify, recall, or choose the offer.

02 / The Principle

Make the position easy to retrieve.

A distinctive name gives the offer a mental handle. Choose the territory first, then make the name reinforce that position instead of forcing the buyer to decode a generic label.

Example

A workflow tool for small agencies might avoid the broad label "project management platform" and name the position it can own: a simple client-approval workspace that makes the next decision visible.

Deliberate practice - 15 minutes

Write the category your offer enters, the position you want it to own, and three possible names. Keep the name that makes the position easiest for a prospect to classify and repeat.

03 / Field Notes

What the field is teaching now.

TechCrunch / 2026-10-13

AI assistants are being framed as a household appliance

What happened: Cerebras CEO Andrew Feldman said consumer AI is moving toward local-first personal assistants that know the user, sit behind a security perimeter, and can eventually act on tasks such as purchases. Why it matters: The positioning shift is from chatbot capability to a new household category. The winning question becomes which mental slot the product can credibly own before every assistant starts sounding interchangeable. Watch: Watch whether products name a specific job or relationship they can own, rather than presenting themselves as another general-purpose AI assistant.

Retail Dive / 2026-10-13

Young shoppers want AI discovery and physical choice

What happened: Retail Dive reports that Gen Z and Gen Alpha are comfortable with AI-led discovery and personalization, but also value stores as sensory, social places that let them escape algorithmic recommendations. Why it matters: A retailer cannot reduce the audience to a single digital-native label. The sharper position may be the bridge between convenient discovery and the freedom to browse, try, and choose in person. Watch: Watch for retail concepts that make the contrast explicit: AI for relevance, stores for surprise, identity, and social experience.

Marketing Dive / 2026-10-13

Chipotle and Unreal turn packaging into cultural merchandise

What happened: Chipotle and Unreal Snacks introduced fashion-oriented bags, joining a wider pattern of food brands using wearable objects to build loyalty and cultural relevance. Why it matters: The object gives the brand a more retrievable handle than a generic food message. It makes the position visible in a context where the category is fashion, not only dining or snacking. Watch: Watch whether the merchandise carries a distinctive brand idea people want to display, or merely turns a logo into another promotional giveaway.

OpenAI hardware competition is becoming a legal and talent story

05 / The Practice

Give the offer a memorable place to stand.

Name the territory, sharpen the mental slot, and remove every word that makes the offer sound like a generic version of someone else's position.
